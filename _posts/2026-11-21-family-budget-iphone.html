Most couples' “system” for money is a group chat, two banking apps and an argument at the end of the month. The apps that promise to fix this usually do it the crude way: one shared account, one shared password, both people logged into the same identity. It works, until it doesn't.

What a family budget actually needs

  • One ledger, two phones. Both partners see the same purchases, bills and envelopes, live.
  • Separate identities. Each person keeps their own Apple Account, their own Face ID, their own device security.
  • Both can write. A budget only works if whoever bought the groceries logs the groceries.
  • One payment. Nobody wants to buy the same app twice.

How Onori does it

Two Apple technologies do the heavy lifting. iCloud sharing (CloudKit) carries the data: you invite your partner from the app, and the household ledger syncs directly between your personal iCloud accounts, with no third-party server in between, ever. Apple Family Sharing carries the purchase: buy Onori Plus once and the members of your Apple family group have it on their own devices.

The result feels almost boring, in the best way: your partner opens the app on their iPhone, everything is just there, and it stays in sync. No invite codes to a company's cloud, no shared login to rotate when a phone gets lost.

One number for two people

A shared ledger is only useful if both people read it the same way. Onori gives the household one number, what you can spend a day until payday, and both phones show the same one. A quick look before buying replaces the conversation about whether there is room for it.

And the privacy part

A household's finances are among the most sensitive data two people share. Everything above happens inside Apple's ecosystem: your devices, your iCloud accounts. Onori has no servers and cannot see a single shared transaction. That's by architecture, not by policy.
